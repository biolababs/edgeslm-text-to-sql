# Abstract

Natural-language access to a local database is a practical use case for small language models, but choosing a configuration requires correctness and device cost measured together. This measurement study applies a reproducible procedure that scores execution correctness and integrated on-board input energy over the same generations on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super with 8 GB of memory, and reports energy per correct answer as the deployment metric. Six small open-weight text-to-SQL models (1B to 4B parameters, 4-bit quantised) are measured over a single-domain taxi database with a rotating query-template split, a curated question set, and the Spider and BIRD development sets, crossed with zero-shot and three-shot prompting. All taxi intervals are 95% percentile bootstraps that resample the 26 query templates the question set is generated from, which is the main reason they are wide; the Spider, BIRD and curated-set accuracies are single-run point estimates with no interval. The interval-backed evidence therefore rests on one database, one session and 26 templates, which is sufficient for the two large within-item treatment effects reported and insufficient to rank artifacts separated by a few points.

Three retrieved similar examples raise strict taxi accuracy from {{qc15-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}–{{qc3-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}% to {{prem1b-q4-ours_nycsim_ex}}–{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycsim_ex}}%, with every template-clustered interval for the lift far above zero. A four-fold rotation requests every item twice under retrieval: once against an example pool containing its own query template and once against a pool from which that template is withheld. Accuracy falls from {{prem1b-q4-ours_seen_ex}}–{{qc3-q4-ollama_seen_ex}}% to {{prem1b-q4-ours_unseen_ex}}–{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_unseen_ex}}%, a within-item gap of {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_gen_gap}}–{{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_gen_gap}} pp whose interval excludes zero for every model. Because the design fixes the two exposures at equal numbers, the pooled lift is the midpoint of two conditional lifts and is not itself a deployment expectation: for QwenCoder-3B the lift is {{qc3-q4-ollama_lift_covered}} pp against a covered pool and {{qc3-q4-ollama_lift_withheld}} pp against a withheld one, pooling to {{qc3-q4-ollama_3shot_lift}} pp. Approximately {{template_share_mean}}% of the zero-shot-to-covered-template improvement (range {{template_share_min}}–{{template_share_max}}%) is associated with the difference between covered and withheld example pools, and once the item's own template is withheld, retrieval's paired advantage over a fixed generic example set separates from zero for only two of the six models — Prem-1B-SQL at {{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}] and Qwen3-4B at {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}] — and is unresolved for the other four, whose intervals all contain zero. Qwen3-4B's margin is itself largely one repeated mistake: {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_n}} of its {{n_nyc_eval}} fixed-example answers fail on a single identical column-name error that no other model makes once.

Energy per correct answer under retrieval spans {{qc15-q4-ollama_nycsim_j_per_correct}}–{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycsim_j_per_correct}} J, so the most accurate configuration was not the cheapest. # 1. Introduction

Text-to-SQL turns a natural-language question into a program that retrieves an answer from a relational database. The appeal is direct: a user asks for a daily revenue total, a comparison between locations, or the busiest period without writing joins and aggregations. An organisation that maintains its own database can keep both questions and schema context inside its own infrastructure by running inference locally. Doing so still requires evidence that the generated queries are correct, that response times are acceptable, and that the model fits the available memory and energy budget.

The task combines language understanding with schema interpretation and executable program generation. Spider made cross-database generalisation a central evaluation target, assembling 10,181 questions and 5,693 distinct complex queries across 200 databases and 138 domains [@yu2018spider]. BIRD then emphasised database values, external knowledge, and execution efficiency, providing 12,751 question–SQL pairs over 95 databases totalling 33.4 GB [@li2023bird]. The two test complementary aspects of database access, and success on either is not a guarantee of reliable querying elsewhere.

Recent findings also challenge a simple division between capable large models and incapable small ones. CodeS-3B reports 78.1% test-suite accuracy on Spider development after supervised fine-tuning, compared with 67.8% in its three-shot setting [@li2024codes]. SLM-SQL reports 67.08% execution accuracy on BIRD development with a 1.5B model, using its training and corrective self-consistency framework [@sheng2025slmsql]. SQL specialisation is therefore a necessary comparison in any study of small-model deployment — but these scores say nothing about what the same models deliver as quantised artifacts on an 8 GB edge board.

The inference procedure matters as much as the model. FINER-SQL reports 67.73% BIRD development execution accuracy and 85.0% Spider development execution accuracy for its 3B variant, with 30 SQL candidates generated per question in its main evaluation [@hoang2026finersql]. A published accuracy score can therefore describe a model-plus-search system whose memory use and latency differ sharply from a single greedy generation. A useful deployment comparison must state candidate count, prompt construction, and any database feedback or reranking alongside parameters and weight precision.

Examples and adaptation offer two ways to specialise a model for a particular database, and they do not simply add up. In DAIL-SQL's LLaMA-7B experiment, Spider development execution accuracy rises from 13.0% at zero-shot to 30.3% with three full-information examples; after supervised fine-tuning, the corresponding values are 66.7% and 61.4% [@gao2024dailsql]. Examples helped before training and hurt after it in that setting, which is a reason to test the combination rather than assume a prompting treatment transfers unchanged.

Generalisation raises a separate concern. Finegan-Dollak et al. show that question-based partitions mainly test new formulations of familiar queries, and propose complementary evaluation on new query structures [@finegandollak2018improving]. In a local deployment a model might answer many paraphrases of a known aggregation while failing on an unfamiliar composition of joins, filters, and subqueries. We therefore separate template availability in the example pool from item identity. Holding out a template measures a controlled form of within-database generalisation; it does not prove that similar SQL was absent from pretraining.

Correctness itself requires careful measurement. Zhong et al. introduce distilled execution test suites to approximate semantic equivalence beyond a single database instance [@zhong2020testsuite]. Jin et al. report annotation errors in 263 of 498 inspected BIRD Mini-Dev examples, or 52.8%, and shifts of up to nine ranking positions when agents are reevaluated on corrected data [@jin2026annotation]. Execution accuracy, test-suite accuracy, and annotation sensitivity are thus worth reporting separately, and a small difference on an unexamined benchmark is weak ground for model selection.

Hardware studies supply relevant but different evidence. Renney et al. report 8.66 tokens/s and 1.16 MJ per million tokens for Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B on the GPU-accelerated Orin Nano Super, using a fixed explanation prompt [@renney2026cloudedge]. Those measurements establish hardware feasibility for a closely related serving setup, but not SQL answer correctness. Edge studies in turn already combine task accuracy with energy on workloads such as MMLU, HellaSwag, and Winogrande [@islam2025slmenergy]. The gap is therefore specific to this SQL deployment setting; we make no broad claim that edge accuracy and energy have never been studied together.

The decision of interest is the cost of obtaining correct answers. Tokens per second cannot show whether a generated query answers the question, and energy per generated token rewards short but wrong outputs. We therefore report the total measured energy spent on an evaluation set divided by its number of strictly correct answers, alongside latency and accuracy. That aggregate includes failed attempts; it does not model an interactive retry process. Reported together, the three quantities let a slower but more accurate configuration be judged on a consistent workload.

Against this background, our study evaluates six small open-weight models on a Jetson Orin Nano Super using a taxi database with a held-out query-template split, a curated question set, and separately labelled public benchmarks, all under one documented serving configuration. We offer it as a measurement study rather than a competitive ranking, and the contributions follow from that. The first is methodological: a documented, re-runnable procedure that scores task correctness and on-board device energy on the same items in the same run, so that a correctness result and an energy result cannot drift apart. The second is the metric and its values: energy per correct answer for six deployed quantised artifacts under two prompting strategies, alongside latency, memory, and per-query energy. The third is a worked demonstration of how uncontrolled comparison misleads, with the corrected-BIRD subset as the case study: the scored denominator moves the numbers more than the annotation correction does. The fourth is a decomposition of the prompting effect. Because every evaluation item is measured both against an example pool that contains its own query template and against one from which that template is withheld, the improvement from zero-shot to a covered pool splits into a component present even when no example matches the required structure and a component associated with template coverage, and the two parts turn out to be of comparable size. The fifth is the model comparison, estimated for all {{n_model_pairs}} pairs rather than asserted from the closest one: {{n_model_pairs_separated}} pairs remain separated after Holm adjustment over the family, several more have unadjusted intervals excluding zero without surviving adjustment, and the closest pairs are genuinely indistinguishable on this evidence. No new modelling method is proposed.

# 2. Related Work

## 2.1. Schema reasoning and valid SQL generation

Before prompt-based systems, relation-aware encoding and constrained decoding addressed two different failure modes. RAT-SQL models relationships among schema elements and question mentions, reporting 57.2% Spider exact-match accuracy and 65.6% with BERT [@wang2020ratsql]. PICARD constrains autoregressive generation by rejecting tokens that cannot continue a valid parse [@scholak2021picard]. These contributions separate understanding the intended database operation from producing valid SQL syntax. Neither syntactic validity nor schema alignment alone establishes that the returned rows answer the question.

This distinction informs our error analysis: an output that cannot be parsed or executed differs from one that executes and returns the wrong rows. Our common prompt requests SQL directly and uses no PICARD-style constrained decoding, so the comparison also measures each model's ability to follow that output contract. Adding constraints would create a different system, to be measured separately.

## 2.2. Specialised small models and inference budgets

CodeS combines SQL-focused incremental pretraining with database prompt construction and domain adaptation. Its Spider development results distinguish execution accuracy from test-suite accuracy: the supervised 3B model reports 83.4% EX and 78.1% TS. Its three-shot BIRD development EX is 31.23% without external knowledge and 39.18% with it [@li2024codes]. Both model preparation and supplied database knowledge move the score, so a published number is not a property of parameter count alone.

SLM-SQL combines supervised training, reinforcement learning, and corrective self-consistency. Its reported BIRD scores include 56.87% EX for 0.5B and 67.08% for 1.5B; the latter appears with 64 generation samples in its cost table, and 16 samples yield 64.84% [@sheng2025slmsql]. A small generator can thus sit inside a computationally substantial inference procedure, which makes its headline score context for specialisation rather than a matched baseline for our single-generation runs.

FINER-SQL uses distillation and fine-grained execution rewards, followed by schema filtering, candidate generation, and voting. Its 3B performance table reports 5.57 seconds per sample in bf16 on a 24 GB A5000, with approximately 10 GB minimum serving VRAM [@hoang2026finersql]. These deployment conditions differ from an 8 GB shared-memory board. We therefore examine SQL-specialised artifacts under our own runtime and prompt conditions rather than infer on-board feasibility from a published model size.

Inference search offers another route. MCTS-SQL reports BIRD development EX rising from 14.71% to 40.69% for its Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B comparison. Its cache experiment reports 0.63 seconds for single-shot generation, 6.12 seconds for uncached search, and 2.84 seconds with prefix caching, at 40.42% cached EX [@yuan2025mctssql]. That is a clear quality–compute trade-off, but without matched Jetson energy measurements.

Together, these papers motivate two controls in our study: include SQL-specialised artifacts, and state the inference procedure. Comparing XiYan with its recorded Qwen base lineage is informative about released-artifact choices, but it does not isolate the causal effect of one training method, and our zero-shot comparison is not a reproduction of the authors' complete systems.

## 2.3. Prompt information, examples, and parameter adaptation

Chang and Fosler-Lussier find that adding foreign-key information to a columns-only representation increases EX from 71.8% to 73.1% for Codex and from 69.1% to 71.2% for ChatGPT. They also observe that the benefit of explicitly stated relationships narrows as in-domain demonstrations increase, while database content remains useful [@chang2023howtoprompt]. A schema-only prompt therefore constitutes a specific information budget, not a neutral representation of everything a deployment could provide.

DAIL-SQL studies example selection, organisation, and fine-tuning; its LLaMA-7B results, quoted above, show demonstrations helping before supervised fine-tuning and hurting after it [@gao2024dailsql]. Our retrieved and fixed example conditions ask two different practical questions: does selecting related questions help, and does a generic set of examples suffice? Because retrieval can preferentially expose familiar structures, the two are compared on paired items.

LoRA freezes the base weights and learns low-rank updates, cutting trainable parameters and optimiser state [@hu2021lora]. It does not remove the memory needed for the base model, activations, or runtime, so feasibility on an edge board remains an empirical question. This paper does not answer it: no adaptation results are reported here, and on-device low-rank adaptation is reserved for the companion study, so parameter adaptation enters only as context for the prompting conditions we do measure.

Recent distillation work reinforces the importance of task scope. SQuaD-SQL describes a Qwen student adapted with synthetic supervision and LoRA on a single 24 GB RTX 4090 [@wu2026squadsql]. Its WikiSQL setting and server-class training memory do not establish multi-table, held-out-template adaptation on the Jetson. Its v1 also uses inconsistent metric terminology and model-size wording, so its headline accuracy is not used as a quantitative comparator here.

## 2.4. Generalisation and benchmark exposure

Finegan-Dollak et al.'s query-based evaluation motivates separating new question wording from new SQL structure [@finegandollak2018improving]. Our template split carries that idea onto a single database: one group of templates is withheld from the example pool and its items are scored as the held-out condition, while items whose templates remain in the pool are scored as the seen condition. That complements rather than replaces Spider's database-disjoint evaluation — a new schema and a new template over a familiar schema test different capabilities — and Section 6.2 reports the resulting contrast, which is large and one-directional for every model here.

Ranaldi et al. compare GPT-3.5 on Spider and on their unfamiliar Termite dataset, and report a substantial decline on the latter [@ranaldi2024contamination]. That is reason to examine contamination risk, not evidence that every model here has seen Spider or a measure of how much contamination contributes. Newly generated taxi questions reduce direct item reuse; public source data and common SQL constructions stop us claiming contamination-free evaluation.

Correlated questions also affect statistical interpretation: many instances of one template do not supply the structural coverage of equally many distinct templates. We therefore keep the template as the resampling unit throughout and report how many templates each estimate rests on — a design choice for our analysis, not a result borrowed from a prior paper. It is also the reason this study's intervals are wide: 449 items drawn from 26 templates carry far less information about structural generalisation than their count suggests.

## 2.5. Semantic metrics and annotation quality

Zhong et al. evaluate distilled test suites on a set of submitted Spider systems and report that the then-current Spider metric produced an average 2.5% false-negative rate and an 8.1% worst-case rate [@zhong2020testsuite]. Those values describe that metric and those submissions, not our evaluator. Test-suite accuracy checks whether different SQL expressions behave equivalently across several database instances; it remains an approximation to semantic correctness.

Jin et al.'s annotation audit and corrected-set reevaluation show that reference quality can move both absolute performance and system ranking [@jin2026annotation]. Their 52.8% figure pertains to the inspected 498-item BIRD Mini-Dev set, not to every BIRD release. We therefore separate corrected-gold rescoring from new inference wherever the question or evidence also changed; otherwise an apparent annotation effect could really be a changed input.

Strict taxi EX, Spider EX, Spider TS, Spider exact-set match, and BIRD Soft F1 therefore stay separate measurements. Public development results give context for local deployment; they do not form a common leaderboard with our taxi task or the Mini-Dev subset. Dataset version, evidence availability, decoding, and candidate count stay attached to every comparison.

## 2.6. Quantization, edge inference, and energy boundaries

Jin et al.'s quantisation evaluation finds that four-bit variants can hold performance close to higher precision on the benchmarks they examine, and that memory reduction need not bring a proportional speed increase [@jin2024quanteval]. Neither result establishes the accuracy effect of GGUF Q4_K_M on SQL generation; a same-source F16/Q8/Q4 ladder, with converter and source weights held fixed, is the test for that and is reserved for the companion study.

MELT evaluates language-model behaviour across mobile and Jetson devices, covering accuracy under quantisation, sustained execution, and energy. Its Jetson experiments show that a lower power mode need not yield lower energy per token, because memory and execution speed change with it [@laskaridis2024melt]. Neither model file size nor nominal power alone determines inference cost.

Renney et al.'s board and model selection match ours closely, which makes their throughput measurements useful hardware context. Their workload is a warm-state, fixed explanation prompt capped at 100 generated tokens, with input power measured externally and no idle subtraction [@renney2026cloudedge]. That per-token energy is not directly comparable with our integrated module-input energy per SQL query.

Islam et al. evaluate accuracy, latency, and energy on MMLU, HellaSwag, and Winogrande using an Otii Ace Pro monitor at 100 Hz [@islam2025slmenergy]. Khatib et al. evaluate 1,531 MMLU prompts across edge-continuum configurations and warn about differing telemetry boundaries and short-output workloads [@khatib2026edgecontinuum]. Combining edge accuracy with energy is therefore not new; our narrower focus is SQL correctness, structural exposure, and the deployment treatments stated below.

LoadGen supplies standard scenario machinery for latency benchmarking [@reddi2020mlperf]. It did not supply any run reported in this paper: the latency and energy figures below come from the accuracy runs themselves, and formal LoadGen SingleStream benchmarking belongs to the companion study. We report the sensor boundary and serving configuration instead, so that task-level trade-offs can be read without conflating benchmarks or measurement systems.

## 2.7. Positioning of this study

The reviewed literature establishes that specialised small models can be capable SQL generators, that examples and inference search change the quality–compute trade-off, and that edge performance depends on the workload and serving system. What these sources do not jointly establish is how the chosen quantised artifacts behave on this board under a documented prompting treatment with correctness linked to on-board energy measured over the same generations. That is the evidence question this campaign addresses, and the template-coverage arm it also carries supplies a control that holds the question fixed while changing whether the example pool covers its structure. That contrast changes the demonstrations as a whole — their wording, their similarity to the question, and the SQL they contain — so it bounds the benefit attributable to pool coverage without isolating any model-internal mechanism. We claim no invention of template withholding, energy normalisation, or small-model text-to-SQL, and the literature search cannot prove the absence of overlapping work.

# 3. Research Questions

The study asks five connected questions:

1. How accurately do the selected small models answer questions over the taxi database, and how does performance differ when query templates are available or absent from the example pool?
2. How do the same artifacts perform on Spider and BIRD under explicitly labelled metrics and dataset versions?
3. How do retrieved and fixed three-shot examples affect accuracy, including on held-out query templates?
4. How do SQL-specialised artifacts compare with general and code-oriented artifacts at similar scales?
5. What are the latency, memory, and energy costs, including energy per correct answer, on the edge board?

Hypotheses and decision thresholds were recorded before the main campaign in `QUESTIONS.md` and `experiment/PLAN.md`, and the analysis below uses those operational definitions, noting deviations where they occur. A zero-shot model has no experiment-defined seen/unseen exposure condition, so its fold-level performance serves only as a difficulty baseline. The template-coverage half of questions 1 and 3 — how accuracy differs when a query template is present in or absent from the example pool — is answered from the within-item paired rotation described in Section 4.3; all five questions are answered with the uncertainty attached. Controlled precision comparison (same-source F16/Q8/Q4) and on-device LoRA fine-tuning are reserved for a companion study on the same infrastructure.

## 4.1. Hardware and serving environment

The test platform is an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super with 8 GB of memory. Preparation and pilot records identify JetPack 6.2.3, L4T R36.5.2, Ollama 0.30.11, and llama.cpp commit `ecdd29e8`. The approved main configuration uses MAXN_SUPER with `jetson_clocks` applied. Test mode pauses the other local services, restricts the inference endpoint to loopback, uses a single parallel request, and configures an F16 KV cache with flash attention. Environment snapshots and model hashes are retained per run.

Five untimed pool requests warm each model after loading. Models are unloaded between blocks, and the amended procedure clears swap and drops caches between models, logging the change. The board ran under ambient laboratory conditions with no active cooling beyond the built-in fan under `nvfancontrol` dynamic control. No ambient temperature sensor was available at the board; idle junction temperature was approximately 53 degrees C at session start.

One serving detail deserves explicit record. Ollama 0.30.11 starts its underlying llama-server without an explicit `--cache-ram` flag and exposes no user-facing setting for it, so the llama.cpp default host-RAM prompt cache was in force throughout session 1, its size not recorded in the session logs because the server exposes no setting for it. That cache raised host-RAM use during long-prompt BIRD runs for models with full multi-head attention (Prem-1B-SQL swap peaked at 936 MB), while NYC taxi runs stayed within memory limits with negligible swap. The NYC energy-per-correct-answer measurements are therefore unaffected; BIRD per-query timing for the affected models carries a swap-pressure caveat. The setting was identical for all six models in the session.

## 4.3. Taxi database and question sets

The local task uses a SQLite database built from a seeded sample of January 2024 NYC yellow taxi records and zone information: 200,000 trip records in three tables. The template generator produces 1,193 question instances across 26 templates, and a seeded allocation reserves {{n_nyc_eval}} instances for evaluation and 744 for the example and adaptation pool. Evaluation items never enter those pools.

Four template folds rotate the held-out structures. For a given fold, the example pool excludes its templates; the items of that fold are then evaluated as the unseen condition and, cyclically, the items of the preceding fold are evaluated against the same pool as the seen condition. Each evaluation item therefore carries one request in which its own template is absent from the pool and one in which it is present, and every request records its pool fold and its exposure role in its own identifier, from which the analysis reads them. All four folds were executed, every evaluation item appears in both roles, and the two roles contribute equal numbers of requests. That pairing is what keeps exposure status from being confounded with question difficulty: the seen and withheld conditions are compared on the same questions rather than on two subsets of the question set. Templates can share component expressions, so withholding a template does not withhold every SQL construct it contains, and the condition is best read as absence of the matching structure from the example pool rather than absence of its parts.

A separate curated (AI-drafted) set contains 92 accepted items, including 15 retained seed items. Questions and two gold-query versions were produced in separate sessions of the same model family; acceptance required execution agreement and rejected degenerate answers, and the team approved a ten-item spot-check before the set was frozen. This gives an additional test set, with the limitation that agreement between related AI systems is weaker validation than independent human annotation.

## 4.4. Public benchmarks and corrected annotations

The public evaluation uses Spider 1.0 development questions and BIRD Mini-Dev v1 in SQLite format. Spider execution accuracy on original databases, distilled test-suite accuracy, and exact-set match are kept distinct. BIRD execution accuracy and Soft F1 are separately labelled, with BIRD evidence included in the prompt.

The corrected-BIRD sensitivity analysis follows the pilot amendment rather than the original assumption that only the SQL changed. The pilot identified 369 items whose question and evidence text were unchanged, so v1 predictions can be rescored on those items to isolate annotation effects from input changes. The full corrected set has 498 items; because some entries have changed inputs or duplicate identifiers, full corrected-set evaluation needs matching predictions and a validated item mapping. Scored denominators follow the protocol: {{n_nyc_eval}} NYC evaluation items, 898 NYC S-similar items (each evaluation item in both seen and unseen roles), {{n_nyc_eval}} NYC S-fixed items, 92 curated items, 1,034 Spider development items, and 500 BIRD Mini-Dev v1 items per model, with corrected-BIRD rescoring on the 498-item Arcwise-Plat-SQL set.

The BIRD evaluator keeps a thirty-second query deadline, and the pilot found gold queries that exceed it. Every BIRD figure below is therefore scored with those items included and counted as the evaluator counts them; a sensitivity analysis excluding the affected questions, and the per-subset accuracy ceiling it would imply, were not computed for this draft and are listed in Section 8 as open work.

## 4.5. Prompts and generation

The common system message asks for one SQLite query, supplies the schema as DDL, and requests SQL without explanation. The zero-shot user message carries the question and, for BIRD, the evidence. Three-shot prompts add three user/assistant question-and-SQL pairs: the primary strategy (S-similar) selects them by deterministic TF-IDF cosine similarity within the permitted pool, ordered so the most similar sits nearest the target question, and the secondary strategy (S-fixed) uses one generic set per pool fold, held fixed across every question in that fold.

Generation is greedy — temperature zero, top-k one, top-p one, repeat penalty one — with a 4,096-token context limit and a 384-token output limit, and overflow and cap hits recorded. The XiYan native prompt is an additional sensitivity condition. The Llama library chat template differs from the model's reference template; we record that serving detail rather than patch it.

SQL extraction removes thinking blocks, prefers a fenced SQL block when present, and otherwise takes the first statement beginning with SELECT or WITH. Each prediction then falls into exactly one of five categories: correct, wrong result (executes but returns incorrect rows), execution error (SQL fails to execute), no SQL extracted, or truncated output (generation cap hit). Secondary flags (swap, cooldown-max) are recorded alongside and do not change that classification.

## 4.6. Scope boundaries

On-device low-rank adaptation and a controlled same-source precision comparison (F16, Q8_0, Q4_K_M) belong to the original protocol but are reserved for a companion study. This paper reports the six Q4_K_M artifacts under one serving configuration and two prompting strategies, and so isolates neither quantisation cost nor fine-tuning effects.

# 5. Measurement and Analysis

## 5.1. Correctness

Strict execution accuracy is the primary taxi metric. Predicted and gold results are compared as row multisets, with ordering enforced for items marked ordered. The protocol's tolerances are stated here in full so that a reader can reproduce the judgement: numeric values match within an absolute difference of 0.01 or a relative difference of 1e-4, strings are compared case-folded and whitespace-trimmed, execution uses a read-only connection with a ten-second query timeout, and at most 10,000 rows are retrieved. Lenient taxi execution accuracy is a secondary figure and is not equivalent to strict execution accuracy or to benchmark Soft F1.

Spider test-suite accuracy uses the pinned evaluator and distilled databases, with the Windows timeout and execution wrappers described in the pilot report. Gold-as-prediction oracle checks and non-null metric checks run before any result enters the paper; Section 6.1 reports their outcome.

## 5.2. Latency and energy

Latency here comes from the accuracy runs: wall time per query, from request send to response receipt, plus the Ollama-reported decode rate. The server also reports a prompt-token rate, but every prompt in a condition shares a long schema prefix that the server caches, so the token count it divides by includes tokens that were never recomputed; the resulting figure implies more arithmetic than the board can perform and is not reported here as a prefill throughput. This is a deviation from the registered protocol, which lists prefill tokens per second among the per-query cost metrics; the quantity is recorded in the raw outputs but withdrawn from the analysis because it cannot be interpreted under a cached shared prefix. No prompt-processing or prefill throughput figure appears anywhere in this paper, and no claim below rests on prompt-processing speed; the decode rate, which divides generated tokens by the server's own generation duration, is unaffected by prefix caching and is the only token-rate figure reported. Ollama's load and prompt-evaluation durations give a server-side time-to-first-token proxy, not a directly observed streaming first-token time.

The decode rate for a condition is the mean over the requests that carry a token count, and two requests across the whole campaign do not. One item in QwenCoder-3B's zero-shot taxi run produced a runaway, degenerate generation, and one item in Prem-1B-SQL's fixed-example taxi run behaved likewise; in both cases the response carried no server timing fields at all, so no decode-token count exists. Those two decode rates are therefore means over all but one of the {{n_nyc_eval}} items rather than over all of them. Every other model-condition combination contributes its full complement of requests. The record is retained in the archive, keeps its wall time and its energy integral, and is scored for correctness like any other item; only the throughput mean omits it. Formal LoadGen SingleStream benchmarking (p50/p90/p99 percentiles with validity checks) is reserved for the companion study.

Input energy is obtained as follows, and the procedure is stated in full because it is what a reader would have to repeat. A background sampler logs the board's on-board VDD_IN power reading with a nanosecond timestamp at a fixed 100 ms interval for the whole session. Each request writes its own send and receive timestamps from the same clock. The scorer then integrates power over the interval between those two timestamps by the trapezoidal rule across every sample inside it, adding a boundary point at each end, each carrying the power of the nearest preceding sample, so that partial intervals at the start and end of generation are not dropped. A window that is not fully covered by samples — no sample before the send timestamp, or none after the receipt timestamp — yields no value and the condition is reported without energy rather than with a truncated integral. The boundary of the measurement is module input as read by the on-board sensors, so wall-plug system energy, including the power supply's own losses, is outside it.

Two energy quantities follow from that integral and both are produced by the analysis: total energy per query (`j_per_query`) and energy above the session's idle baseline (`j_above_idle`), the latter subtracting the median idle VDD_IN power of the session-start baseline multiplied by the query's wall time. **Every energy value quoted in this paper, including every energy-per-correct-answer figure, is total energy.** The above-idle values are computed by the scorer for every condition and carried into the analysis output for the four taxi and curated conditions, where they are available for readers who want the marginal cost of a query on an already-powered board, but they are not carried into the energy table or quoted in the text, so no figure below mixes the two definitions. The measured window is generation only: it opens when the request is sent and closes when the response is received, so example retrieval, SQL extraction, and SQL execution against the database all happen outside it and none of their cost is counted here.

For the taxi accuracy runs, energy per correct answer is the total energy spent on all evaluated items divided by the number answered strictly correctly, and so includes energy spent on wrong answers. With no correct answers the ratio is undefined and is labelled as such. Following the approved pilot amendment, correctness and energy are paired within the accuracy runs, so the same generation produces both.

Peak RAM, swap behaviour, temperatures, clock behaviour, and power alarms accompany the task metrics. The amended rule separates recorded over-current alarms from throttling flags, which rest on clock drops or the specified temperature threshold. Suspect measurements stay in the evidence archive and receive an explicit inclusion or exclusion decision.

## 5.3. Statistical comparisons

The registered analysis specifies Wilson intervals for item-level proportions and cluster bootstrap intervals for the relevant dependencies: templates for the generated taxi set, databases for Spider and BIRD. This section states what the analysis script actually does, because every estimate and interval reported below depends on those choices.

**Cluster bootstrap over templates.** Every interval quoted for the taxi set is a 95% percentile bootstrap in which the unit resampled with replacement is the query template — and there are 26 of them — not the item. That cluster count is the single most important number for reading any taxi interval in this paper, and the main reason the intervals are as wide as they are: an interval on this question set rests on 26 independent units, not on {{n_nyc_eval}}. Twenty-six templates are drawn with replacement, all the items belonging to each drawn template are taken with it, and the statistic is recomputed; the quoted bounds are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 10,000 such replicates under the fixed seed 20261017, so any reader re-running the script obtains the same bounds. The template is the unit for two reasons. First, the 449 evaluation items are generated from 26 templates, so items sharing a template share their SQL skeleton and are strongly correlated: treating them as 449 independent Bernoulli trials would understate the true uncertainty by a large factor, because the effective sample size is closer to the number of templates than to the number of items. Second, the S-similar condition contributes two measurements per item — each of the 449 items is requested once against a pool containing its template and once against a pool without it, giving 898 rows — so those rows are repeated measurements of the same item and must not be resampled independently; resampling whole templates keeps an item's two rows together. Because the rotation puts every template's items into both exposure roles, each cluster carries rows from both conditions, so a resampled set of templates always contains both and the coverage-gap statistic is defined on every replicate. The consequence is visible in the tables: intervals on a single condition are several points wide, and intervals on a difference between conditions or models are wider still.

**Primary inference for every taxi contrast.** The template cluster bootstrap described above is the primary inference for all of them: the prompting contrasts, the template-coverage contrast, the decomposition share, the model-against-model contrasts, and energy per correct answer. Each is formed as a difference or a ratio of resampled quantities, resampled over templates, and reported as an interval. The model comparison is formed per request on the items both models answered, and template clustering is what keeps an item's two rows together under resampling.

**Pooled and conditional three-shot lifts.** Three different lifts can be formed from the retrieval condition, and the analysis reports all three because they answer different questions. The covered lift compares zero-shot accuracy with accuracy against an example pool that contains the item's own query template; the withheld lift compares zero-shot accuracy with accuracy against a pool from which that template has been removed; the pooled lift compares zero-shot accuracy with the mean of the two. The pooled lift is therefore the arithmetic midpoint of the other two by construction, because the four-fold rotation requests every item exactly once in each role and the condition contains the two exposures in equal numbers. That one-half ratio is a property of the experimental design and of nothing else: a deployment's example pool covers whatever share of incoming questions it happens to cover, and the pooled lift would move with that share. The pooled value is reported for continuity with the headline S-similar accuracy, and wherever a per-model pooled lift is the figure under discussion it is accompanied by the two conditional values it sits between.

**Model-contrast family and multiplicity.** All {{n_model_pairs}} pairs of the six models are estimated under the S-similar condition, each paired per request and clustered by template, and the resulting bootstrap p-values are adjusted by the Holm–Bonferroni procedure across the family of {{n_model_pairs}}. The contrast table carries two interval columns for each pair, and the distinction matters for how a row is read. The per-pair interval is the ordinary 95% percentile interval for that one comparison, which is the right quantity if a reader cares about a single pre-chosen pair. The simultaneous interval is the same bootstrap distribution read at Bonferroni-level percentiles for a family of {{n_model_pairs}}, so that the whole set of {{n_model_pairs}} intervals covers its targets jointly with 95% confidence. Only the simultaneous column is logically consistent with the Holm decision: reporting a per-pair interval that excludes zero next to an adjusted verdict of "not separated" states two things that contradict each other at a glance, which is why both are shown with their coverage levels named rather than one being quietly substituted for the other. Section 6.2 reads the separation claims off the simultaneous column and treats the per-pair column as the unadjusted, single-comparison view.

**Adjustment is applied to one family only.** The model-contrast family of {{n_model_pairs}} pairs is the only set of comparisons in this paper that receives a multiplicity adjustment. The six zero-shot-to-S-similar lifts, the six fixed-example lifts, the six template-coverage gaps, the six coverage shares, and the six energy-per-correct-answer intervals are all reported as unadjusted 95% intervals, one per model, with no correction for the fact that six models are examined. For the coverage gaps and the retrieved-example lifts this makes little practical difference, because those effects are tens of percentage points and every bound is far from zero; for the fixed-example lifts, where one interval already includes zero and the nearest of the others has a lower bound of {{prem1b-q4-ours_fixlift_ci_lo}} pp, an adjustment over six would widen the intervals and could move a further case to unresolved. Readers should treat those as nominal single-comparison intervals, not as family-wise statements.

**Exact McNemar tests are supplementary.** Two contrasts are paired at the item level — zero-shot against three fixed examples, and the template-coverage contrast — and for each the analysis matches the two measurements of every item, counts the discordant pairs, and reports the exact binomial two-sided p-value with no normal approximation and no continuity correction. These tests are reported as a supplementary item-level description, not as primary evidence. They handle the dependence between the two measurements of one item, but they treat items as independent, which they are not: the {{n_nyc_eval}} items are generated from 26 templates and items sharing a template share their SQL skeleton. A McNemar p-value computed this way therefore rests on an independence assumption the design violates, and its magnitude should not be read as a measure of strength of evidence. The template-clustered intervals above are what carry the inferential weight for these contrasts.

**What is not in this draft.** Two quantities the protocol specifies remain absent from the script rather than merely from the tables: database-cluster intervals for Spider and BIRD, whose accuracies appear as single-run point estimates with no interval of any kind; and any interval on the curated-set accuracies, which were not computed even though a small sample is a reason to quantify uncertainty rather than to omit it. The missing benchmark intervals constrain Section 6.2, and no separation between adjacent Spider or BIRD values is claimed there. Every table is populated from the scored outputs, and every result number in the text is substituted from the analysis output rather than typed; design constants such as item counts, the template count and the bootstrap settings are written as literals.

### RQ3: Three-shot prompting

Supplying examples in the prompt is the treatment this design estimates most precisely, together with the template coverage examined above, and the two are connected at the end of this subsection. The pooled figure sits exactly between each pair by construction, because the rotation requests every item once in each role and the condition therefore contains the two exposures in equal numbers: QwenCoder-3B is {{qc3-q4-ollama_lift_covered}} pp covered, {{qc3-q4-ollama_lift_withheld}} pp withheld, and {{qc3-q4-ollama_3shot_lift}} pp pooled. **That one-half exposure ratio was fixed by the design and corresponds to nothing in deployment**, so the pooled lift should not be read as what a deployment would gain; the covered and withheld values bracket the range it could take, and where in that range a particular deployment falls is set by how much of its question distribution its pool already covers.

Three generic examples do less, and unevenly so, and here the clustered intervals change the conclusion. QwenCoder-1.5B gains {{qc15-q4-ollama_fixlift}} pp under S-fixed ({{qc15-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}% to {{qc15-q4-ollama_nycfix_ex}}%) with an interval of [{{qc15-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_lo}}, {{qc15-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_hi}}], whereas Qwen3-4B moves only {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_fixlift}} pp ({{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}% to {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_ex}}%) with an interval of [{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_lo}}, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_hi}}] — **an interval that includes zero**. For Qwen3-4B, therefore, this evidence does not establish that a generic example set helps at all. The other five intervals do exclude zero: {{prem1b-q4-ours_fixlift}} pp [{{prem1b-q4-ours_fixlift_ci_lo}}, {{prem1b-q4-ours_fixlift_ci_hi}}] for Prem-1B-SQL, {{qc3-q4-ollama_fixlift}} pp [{{qc3-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_lo}}, {{qc3-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_hi}}] for QwenCoder-3B, {{xiyan3b-q4-ours_fixlift}} pp [{{xiyan3b-q4-ours_fixlift_ci_lo}}, {{xiyan3b-q4-ours_fixlift_ci_hi}}] for XiYanSQL-3B, and {{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_fixlift}} pp [{{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_lo}}, {{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_hi}}] for Llama-3.2-3B. The claim this draft supports is accordingly that fixed generic examples help five of the six models and are not resolved for the sixth, while retrieved examples help all six. That is a statement about each strategy against zero-shot, not a statement that relevance beats mere presence; the next paragraph makes the direct retrieval-against-fixed comparison and finds it much less one-sided. Qwen3-4B is not the model with the highest zero-shot taxi accuracy — that is QwenCoder-3B at {{qc3-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}% against Qwen3-4B's {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}% — so the unresolved fixed-example effect is not simply a ceiling artifact. It does have an explanation, and the next paragraph but one gives it: nearly half of Qwen3-4B's fixed-example answers fail on one repeated column-name error.

The comparison that matters for choosing between the two strategies is not retrieval against zero-shot but retrieval against a fixed set, and it has to be made in the condition a deployment will mostly be in: one where the incoming question's own structure is not already sitting in the pool. Made that way, retrieval's advantage is mostly unresolved. The two conditions score the same {{n_nyc_eval}} questions once each, so the difference is formed within items and then resampled over query templates like every other taxi contrast in this paper. On that footing, the advantage separates from zero for two of the six models: Prem-1B-SQL at {{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}] and Qwen3-4B at {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}]. For the other four the interval contains zero and the comparison is simply unresolved: QwenCoder-3B at {{qc3-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{qc3-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{qc3-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}], XiYanSQL-3B at {{xiyan3b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{xiyan3b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{xiyan3b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}], Llama-3.2-3B at {{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}], and QwenCoder-1.5B at {{qc15-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{qc15-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{qc15-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}]. QwenCoder-1.5B deserves explicit comment because its point estimate is the one negative value in the set: its interval spans zero comfortably, so this study does **not** find retrieval worse than generic examples for that model, and an earlier draft's reading of it as a loss was an artifact of counting signs. Counting signs is in general the wrong operation here — four of the six point estimates are positive, but on these 26 clusters only two of the six differences are distinguishable from no difference at all. The reading we take from this is that retrieval's advantage over a fixed example set is, on this evidence, mostly an effect of the pool covering the item's template rather than of similarity-based selection as such: once coverage is removed, four of the six comparisons cannot tell the two strategies apart, and of the two that can, one is explained in large part by a single failure mode in the fixed condition rather than by any strength of retrieval. That is the interval-based form of the conclusion and it is better supported than the sign count it replaces.

Qwen3-4B's behaviour in the fixed-example condition needs that separate explanation, because without it the model's {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_ex}}% score invites the wrong inference. Of the {{n_nyc_eval}} fixed-example items, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_n}} — {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_pct}}% of the whole set — fail with one identical database error: the model writes the zone-table join column as `pz.location` where the schema names it `pz.location_id`, and SQLite rejects the query. The other five models produce that error zero times in this condition. The prompt is not at fault: the fixed demonstration set names the column correctly several times over and never in the erroneous form, so the model is not copying the mistake from its examples. Nor is it a harness artifact or a token cap — every one of those generations terminated with a normal stop reason rather than at the 384-token output limit of Section 4.5, and the scorer classified none of the items in this condition as truncated. It is a reproducible single-token lexical slip, repeated on nearly half the condition.

Three consequences follow, and they bound how the fixed-example result should be read. First, Qwen3-4B's low fixed-example score reflects one repeated lexical error on nearly half the set, not a broad inability to use generic examples: on the items where it does not make this slip the model is not conspicuously weak. Second, its large retrieval advantage under withheld templates — the {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp above, one of the two that separate from zero — is therefore substantially attributable to retrieval supplying examples that happen to avoid this failure, rather than to similarity-based selection being generally superior. Third, and generally: when a single-token column-name slip dominates a condition, that condition's score should be read as a failure mode rather than as a capability estimate, and any contrast computed against it inherits the same caveat. For scale, the largest dominant error mode anywhere else in the two taxi conditions the analysis scans is well below this: {{qc15-q4-ollama_nyc0_top_error_pct}}% for QwenCoder-1.5B and {{prem1b-q4-ours_nyc0_top_error_pct}}% for Prem-1B-SQL, both at zero-shot, and no other model-condition has any single error message accounting for as much as a tenth of its set. That scan covers the taxi zero-shot and fixed-example conditions only; the retrieval conditions, the curated set, and the public benchmarks were not examined for repeated error modes, so this is not an exhaustive survey of such failures in the campaign.

How much of the retrieval gain is associated with template coverage rather than with having relevant examples at all is the natural follow-up, and the fold rotation gives a quantitative answer. The first step is worth {{qc3-q4-ollama_example_effect}} pp for QwenCoder-3B at the low end and {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_example_effect}} pp for Qwen3-4B at the high end — these are the withheld-pool lifts listed earlier in this subsection, reached by a different route — and the second is worth {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_template_effect}}–{{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_template_effect}} pp.

Against the zero-shot-to-covered-pool improvement, the coverage step supplies a mean of {{template_share_mean}}% (range {{template_share_min}}% for Qwen3-4B to {{template_share_max}}% for Llama-3.2-3B): approximately half of the zero-shot-to-covered-template improvement is associated with the difference between covered and withheld example pools. What the treatment actually changed is worth recording, because it bounds the interpretation. Of the {{shots_seen_n}} requests made against a covered pool, {{shots_seen_all_same}} carried three demonstrations drawn from the item's own template, and {{shots_seen_none_same}} carried none. Of the {{shots_unseen_n}} requests made against a withheld pool, {{shots_unseen_none_same}} carried none — that is, every one of them. The split is model-dependent — the artifact with the highest observed pooled S-similar accuracy shows the smallest coverage share, although the ordering differs if models are ranked by covered-pool accuracy instead — though with six models and one session that pattern is an observation rather than a result.

{{table:error_taxonomy}}

![**Figure 7. Where zero-shot predictions fail.** Composition of the 449 zero-shot taxi predictions per model. Low zero-shot accuracy can fail in two quite different ways, and the taxonomy separates them. In this zero-shot taxi condition every model emits extractable SQL on every item — the no-SQL column is zero — so the failures divide between queries that execute and return the wrong rows, queries that do not execute at all, and a handful of truncated outputs. That does not hold everywhere: Prem-1B-SQL yielded no extractable statement on one BIRD item and on two Spider items. Figure 7 shows that the balance differs by model. Qwen3-4B produces the fewest execution errors and the most wrong results: its SQL is valid and misdirected. QwenCoder-1.5B is the opposite, with the highest execution-error count, and simply struggles to write valid SQL without examples. Prem-1B-SQL has the highest truncation count at seven items, with the other models at two or fewer, consistent with occasional generation beyond the 384-token cap. QwenCoder-3B, with the highest observed zero-shot score at {{qc3-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}%, combines a low execution-error count with a high wrong-result count, which is the profile of a model producing plausible but still incorrect queries. For a deployment this matters: a query that executes and returns wrong rows is harder for a user to detect than one that raises an error.

The table and Figure 7 both cover the zero-shot condition only, and that restriction hides the single largest error concentration in the campaign. Qwen3-4B's zero-shot profile — the fewest execution errors of the six — reverses under fixed examples, where {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_n}} of {{n_nyc_eval}} items ({{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_pct}}%) fail on the one repeated `pz.location` column-name error analysed in RQ3. That is not an unexplained outlier in the error profile but a single reproducible lexical slip, and it is the reason this paper treats Qwen3-4B's fixed-example score as a failure mode rather than as a capability estimate. Within the zero-shot condition shown here, the comparable concentrations are far smaller: QwenCoder-1.5B's largest single execution-error message accounts for {{qc15-q4-ollama_nyc0_top_error_pct}}% of its items and Prem-1B-SQL's for {{prem1b-q4-ours_nyc0_top_error_pct}}%, and no other model-condition among the two scanned reaches a tenth of its set. A per-condition taxonomy for the two three-shot conditions is not computed in this draft and is listed in Section 8.

# 7. Discussion

Two findings in this campaign are estimated precisely enough to carry weight, and both concern the prompt rather than the model. The first is the size of the retrieval effect: every model gains at least {{qc3-q4-ollama_3shot_lift}} percentage points on the taxi set from three retrieved examples, QwenCoder-1.5B gains {{qc15-q4-ollama_3shot_lift}} points, and every paired template-cluster interval for those lifts is far above zero. Those pooled gains are the midpoint of two very different conditional gains and should always be quoted as the pair: QwenCoder-3B gains {{qc3-q4-ollama_lift_covered}} pp when the example pool contains the item's own query template and {{qc3-q4-ollama_lift_withheld}} pp when it does not, pooling to {{qc3-q4-ollama_3shot_lift}} pp, and Qwen3-4B gains {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_lift_covered}} pp against {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_lift_withheld}} pp, pooling to {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_3shot_lift}} pp. The pooled value falls where it does because the rotation made the two exposures equally frequent, not because that is what a deployment will see. Fixed generic examples are the weaker treatment against zero-shot and not uniformly effective: five of the six clustered intervals exclude zero, but Qwen3-4B's is [{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_lo}}, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_hi}}] pp and includes zero, so the claim that fixed examples help every model is not supported by this evidence. That one exception now has a concrete cause rather than standing as an anomaly: {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_pct}}% of Qwen3-4B's fixed-example answers fail on a single repeated column-name error (`pz.location` for `pz.location_id`) that no other model commits once, so its fixed-example score is better read as a failure mode than as a measure of how well it uses generic examples. The second finding is the composition of the retrieval effect. Withholding the item's own query template from the pool costs {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_gen_gap}}–{{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_gen_gap}} pp on the same items, with every cluster-bootstrap interval excluding zero; approximately {{template_share_mean}}% of the zero-shot-to-covered-template improvement is associated with the difference between covered and withheld example pools, and against the pooled S-similar headline the same component is {{template_share_pooled_mean}}%. Among the models, the field is partially ordered: {{n_model_pairs_separated}} of {{n_model_pairs}} pairs separate after Holm adjustment, both of them Prem-1B-SQL against a 3B artifact, while the leading group is not separated internally.

The benchmark comparison is a weaker but still practical observation. Prem-1B-SQL records the highest observed execution accuracy on both Spider and BIRD, the benchmarks its specialisation targets, and sits second-lowest on the zero-shot taxi set, while QwenCoder-3B shows the opposite profile. XiYanSQL-3B improves markedly on its QwenCoder-3B base on the public benchmarks under the common prompt, by {{xiyan3b-q4-ours_spider_ex}} against {{qc3-q4-ollama_spider_ex}}% on Spider execution accuracy and {{xiyan3b-q4-ours_bird_ex}} against {{qc3-q4-ollama_bird_ex}}% on BIRD, but not on the taxi database, where it is level under retrieval and lower at zero-shot. With six models the rank correlation is uninformative, so we do not claim a general dissociation between benchmark and local performance. What the reversal does show is that a leaderboard position is not sufficient evidence for a private-database deployment, because at least sometimes it points the wrong way, and the cost of measuring on the target database is one session of the procedure described here.

The size of the prompting effect bears on deployment architecture, with one honest caveat about cost. A system that retrieves similar past queries before generating SQL scored {{prem1b-q4-ours_nycsim_ex}}–{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycsim_ex}}% on this workload against {{qc15-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}–{{qc3-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}% for zero-shot generation, and that difference is far larger than any difference we observed between models. Those pooled figures bracket two regimes a deployment would experience separately: {{prem1b-q4-ours_seen_ex}}–{{qc3-q4-ollama_seen_ex}}% where the pool already covers the incoming question's structure and {{prem1b-q4-ours_unseen_ex}}–{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_unseen_ex}}% where it does not, so the right planning figure depends on how much of the expected question distribution the pool covers. We cannot say what the retrieval infrastructure costs: the measured window covers generation only, so the time, energy, and engineering effort of building and maintaining a validated pool of question–SQL pairs, and of retrieving from it, were not measured in this study at all. We can say how such a pool behaves when it does not cover a user's question, because that is the withheld condition. The defensible claim is therefore narrower than the headline: on this workload, three retrieved demonstrations produced accuracy gains larger than every model-pair difference measured here, including the {{n_model_pairs_separated}} of {{n_model_pairs}} that the design separates, conditional on access to a pool of validated examples whose own cost is unmeasured, and with approximately half of the zero-shot-to-covered-template improvement associated with the pool actually covering the incoming question's structure.

A deployment is not, however, choosing between retrieval and an empty prompt. It is choosing between a retrieval pipeline — an indexed, maintained, validated pool plus a similarity search on every request — and three good generic examples pasted into the system message once. On that comparison the case for retrieval is much weaker than the zero-shot contrast suggests, and it weakens precisely in the regime that matters. With the item's own template withheld from the pool, retrieval's paired advantage over the fixed set separates from zero for two of the six models and is unresolved for the other four. The two are Prem-1B-SQL at {{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}] and Qwen3-4B at {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}]. For QwenCoder-3B, XiYanSQL-3B, Llama-3.2-3B, and QwenCoder-1.5B the interval contains zero, so no direction is established for any of them; in particular QwenCoder-1.5B's negative point estimate of {{qc15-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp carries the interval [{{qc15-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{qc15-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}], and this study therefore does not find retrieval worse than generic examples for that model either. Of the two separated cases, Qwen3-4B's is the one to discount: its fixed-example score is depressed by the repeated `pz.location` column error that accounts for {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_pct}}% of that condition's items (Section 6.2), so its retrieval margin measures retrieval avoiding one specific failure rather than similarity-based selection being generally better. We therefore do not claim that retrieval is the stronger prompting strategy in general. What this evidence supports is narrower, and now rests on intervals rather than on a count of signs: retrieval is clearly stronger than no examples, and stronger than a fixed set when the pool covers the incoming question's structure, while on structurally novel questions its advantage over a fixed set is established for one model on general grounds, for a second only through that model's fixed-condition failure mode, and not established at all for the remaining four. On this workload, retrieval's edge over a fixed example set looks mostly like an effect of template coverage rather than of similarity-based selection as such — which is also the reason Figure 2 shows the four conditions separately rather than pooling the retrieval arm.

That conditionality is the most important qualification of the retrieval result, and in this study it has a quantitative answer rather than a hedge. The taxi questions are generated from templates and the example pool is drawn from the same generator, so a retrieved neighbour can share not only the required SQL structure but much of the question's wording — which is precisely the confound the withheld condition was built to control. Excluding the item's own template from the pool removes the neighbour that instantiates the required skeleton and leaves only examples built from other structures, and it also lowers the measured similarity of the retrieved examples, from {{shots_seen_sim_mean}} to {{shots_unseen_sim_mean}} on average. Under that contrast, approximately half of the zero-shot-to-covered-template improvement is associated with the difference between covered and withheld example pools: a mean of {{template_share_mean}}% across the six models, from {{template_share_min}}% to {{template_share_max}}%. Measured instead against the pooled S-similar headline, which averages the two exposures in equal numbers, the same component is {{template_share_pooled_mean}}% (range {{template_share_pooled_min}}–{{template_share_pooled_max}}%); the two shares answer different questions and the pooled one is the correct denominator whenever the headline S-similar figure is the point of reference. Either way, a substantial part of the gain goes away when the pool does not cover the incoming structure. The remaining gain is the quantity a deployment should plan around, since users do not restrict themselves to the structures already in the pool, and it is the one a benchmark built from generated questions and a matching example pool will systematically flatter.

The curated set points the same way, and if anything further. Its questions were drafted separately and are not template instances, so no pool entry is another instance of the same generator rule, and on it Qwen3-4B moves from {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_cur0_ex}}% zero-shot to {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_cursim_ex}}% with retrieval — a gain several times smaller than the {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_3shot_lift}} pp it gains on the generated taxi set in pooled terms ({{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_lift_covered}} pp covered, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_lift_withheld}} pp withheld), and smaller even than the {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_lift_withheld}} pp that remains on the taxi set once the matching template is withheld. That last comparison is not clean, because the curated zero-shot baseline is far higher ({{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_cur0_ex}}% against {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}%) and leaves less room to gain. The curated set has 92 items; no interval was computed for it in this draft, which is an omission rather than a consequence of its size, since a small sample is a reason to quantify uncertainty and not to leave it unstated. The two contrasts agree in direction without agreeing in magnitude: both say that part of the taxi retrieval gain is specific to a workload whose questions and examples come from one generator, and the template-coverage decomposition is the one of the two that measures that part on the same items under a paired design.

Two of the three controls we would want are still missing. Random examples drawn from the same pool would vary example relevance while holding example presence fixed, separating "a similar example" from "any example" — the S-fixed condition is a partial substitute, since it holds presence fixed with examples that are not selected for relevance, and the comparison above between withheld-pool retrieval and S-fixed is as close as this design comes to that control; a within-pool random condition would do it cleanly, on the same pool and the same item distribution. Human paraphrases of the questions would break wording overlap without changing the required SQL, separating the lexical from the structural part of the coverage effect, which the template contrast cannot do on its own. The third control — retrieval that excludes the target question's own template — is the withheld condition and is reported above. Until the other two are run, the retrieval result should be read as an effect of supplying relevant examples on a template-generated workload, with approximately half of the zero-shot-to-covered-template improvement associated with pool coverage and the lexical and structural components of that half not separated. Nothing here identifies a mechanism inside the model.

The swap flags recorded in Section 6.1 indicate that 8 GB is tight for the larger Q4_K_M artifacts once the Ollama server, prompt context, and KV cache are counted. A deployment should monitor memory pressure and decide whether swap-induced latency is acceptable.

The corrected-BIRD rescoring is instructive in a different way than expected. Scored on the items whose inputs are unchanged, corrected gold moves every model by a small amount and mostly downwards, and that is enough to split the two models that tie on the subset under v1 gold; the much larger shifts appear when the scored subset changes. Annotation quality is therefore a real but modest effect here, while the subset and dataset version are the dominant bookkeeping risk in any comparison with published BIRD figures.

The energy data expose a trade-off that accuracy alone hides, and it is the part of the measurement we would most encourage others to reuse. Under S-similar prompting, QwenCoder-1.5B costs {{qc15-q4-ollama_nycsim_j_per_correct}} J per correct answer against {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycsim_j_per_correct}} J for Qwen3-4B, at a lower accuracy ({{qc15-q4-ollama_nycsim_ex}}% against {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycsim_ex}}%) whose difference this evaluation does not resolve. The useful consequence is structural rather than a recommendation of one artifact: the two axes behave alike, separating the extremes of the field and leaving adjacent configurations unseparated, so neither axis supports a complete ordering of the six. The energy intervals quoted in Section 6.3 make that explicit — QwenCoder-1.5B's lies wholly below Qwen3-4B's, while QwenCoder-3B's and XiYanSQL-3B's almost coincide — and they cover workload variation only, with no estimate of run-to-run variation from a single session. A deployment that can tolerate occasional wrong answers therefore has a measurable reason to prefer QwenCoder-1.5B, the smallest-footprint artifact, over the largest, and no measured reason here to prefer one 3B artifact over another on either axis. We deliberately do not recommend a model or a migration path: this study tested neither a staged deployment nor any model outside the six listed. A reader wanting a recommendation should run the procedure on their own database and questions, which is what the reproducibility package is for.

# 8. Limitations

The study uses one edge board and one principal single-domain database, so it cannot speak to other devices, power modes, databases, or concurrent-user workloads. Template withholding measures a controlled form of within-database generalisation, and no more than that: templates share component expressions and pretraining exposure is unknown, so the withheld condition shows what happens when the matching structure is absent from the example pool, not what happens when it is absent from the model. It is a treatment on the example pool, not an isolation of a model-internal mechanism, and it changes example wording, similarity, and SQL content together, so lexical and structural explanations of the gap are not separated. The rank-correlation comparison rests on six models and carries correspondingly wide uncertainty.

The curated questions and both gold-query versions were AI-generated within one model family, with a limited human spot-check, so their agreement does not rule out shared annotation errors. The public benchmarks carry their own annotation and timeout limitations, and execution equivalence on a finite set of databases remains an imperfect proxy for semantic correctness. The BIRD evaluator's thirty-second deadline affects gold queries identified in the pilot, and no sensitivity analysis excluding those items was computed for this draft, so the BIRD figures should be read as the evaluator's own verdict including them.

The common prompt supports a controlled artifact comparison but is not optimal for every model. Quantised releases can differ in conversion details, and comparing artifacts does not isolate training differences. Latency is conditional on the serving and cache configuration, and onboard VDD_IN energy excludes wall-plug consumption. The experiment covers single-query generation only, with no interactive clarification and no retrying agent.

Three statistical limitations should be read with every number above. First, multiplicity adjustment is applied to the model-contrast family only; the six retrieved-example lifts, the six fixed-example lifts, the six coverage gaps, the six coverage shares, and the six energy-per-correct-answer intervals are nominal single-comparison intervals with no correction for examining six models. Second, the comparison of withheld-pool retrieval against fixed examples — the comparison that qualifies the retrieval result most sharply — now carries paired template-clustered intervals, and they are wide: four of the six contain zero, so only two of the six differences are resolved and the per-model ordering of the six point estimates should not be read closely. Those six intervals are also uncorrected for having examined six models, so the two that exclude zero would be the ones to re-examine first under a multiplicity adjustment. Relatedly, the error-mode scan behind the Qwen3-4B explanation covers the taxi zero-shot and fixed-example conditions only and reports a mode only when it reaches a tenth of the condition's items; a per-condition error taxonomy for the two three-shot conditions, which would show whether other repeated lexical failures are hiding inside the retrieval scores, is not computed in this draft. Third, no prefill or prompt-processing throughput is reported anywhere in this paper. The server's prompt-token rate is uninterpretable under a shared, cached schema prefix, so prompt-side speed is simply not measured here. The per-query wall times in Section 6.3 contain whatever prompt processing each request required but do not isolate it, and no claim in this paper rests on how fast the board ingests a prompt.

This paper reports accuracy and per-query costs without formal LoadGen latency percentiles, a controlled same-source precision comparison, or on-device LoRA results; all three belong to the companion study, and no precision ladder or adaptation cost is measured here. The latency and energy figures come from the accuracy runs themselves, and no LoadGen run contributed to any number reported above. Model-to-model differences are confirmed for {{n_model_pairs_separated}} of the {{n_model_pairs}} pairs after Holm adjustment and should be treated as unconfirmed for the rest, several of which have unadjusted intervals excluding zero and would repay a larger template inventory. The swap pressure recorded for three models is flagged but not excluded, and a deployment under sustained swap may behave differently.

## 8.1. What this design cannot settle

What this evidence leaves open is bounded by four features of the design, and they should be read together rather than as a list of caveats.

The first and largest is the template inventory. The whole generated taxi set rests on 26 distinct query structures, and because items sharing a template share their SQL skeleton, the effective sample for any structural question is far closer to 26 than to the {{n_nyc_eval}} items. That is why the template-coverage gap, which is tens of percentage points, clears the resampling scheme easily while a model-to-model difference of a point or two does not: the two highest-scoring configurations differ by {{top2_diff}} pp, an estimate whose unadjusted per-pair interval [{{top2_ci_lo}}, {{top2_ci_hi}}] already spans zero and widens further when the interval is made simultaneous over the {{n_model_pairs}}-pair family. A wider inventory would also tell us whether the coverage gap is stable across kinds of query structure; with 26 templates we can estimate the gap precisely without describing how it varies with query complexity. The decomposition does carry intervals — Section 6.2 reports a template-clustered interval on the coverage share for each model — but those intervals are wide, and the across-model spread of the share ({{template_share_min}}–{{template_share_max}}% against the covered-pool denominator) should not be read as an ordering.

The second is the construction of the questions. Every taxi question is generated from a template, so a question's wording resembles the wording of other instances of the same template by construction, and the example pool is drawn from the same generator. The withheld condition changes pool coverage but not the generator's style: in both conditions the target question and its retrieved neighbours come from one question-writing process. A gap measured this way is the effect of having the matching structure in the pool on a machine-generated question set, and it would need human-written questions to show the same magnitude on natural phrasing. It also cannot say which feature of the changed demonstrations — their wording, their similarity, or their SQL — produced the gap.

The third is the size and provenance of the independent set. The curated set, which is the only non-template question set on the local database, contains 92 accepted items, drafted and gold-labelled by related AI systems with a limited human spot-check. No interval was computed for its accuracies in this draft; that is an omission in the analysis, not a consequence of the set's size, and a 92-item sample is precisely a case where an interval would be informative. The set is also related enough to its own annotator that it is weaker validation than independent human annotation. It supports the direction of the discussion in Section 7 and not a second quantitative estimate of the coverage effect.

The fourth is the single session. One board, one serving configuration, one scored run per condition, and greedy decoding throughout: accuracy has no repeat measurement, so run-to-run variation is assumed rather than observed, and nothing here separates residual decoding or scheduling non-determinism from a genuine difference. The same applies to the energy figures, which are single-session integrals on one board under uncontrolled laboratory ambient conditions, with one cooldown-max flag and three swap flags recorded and retained. The template-clustered intervals reported on energy per correct answer therefore describe variation across the workload and not across sessions; the latter is unmeasured.

Four additions would narrow what is left, in rough order of value per unit of effort. First, widen the template inventory substantially; the binding constraint is the number of distinct query structures, so adding instances to the existing 26 templates would narrow nothing. Second, add human-written or human-paraphrased questions over the same schema, which would break the shared wording between question and pool and show how much of the measured coverage effect is lexical rather than structural — the control the withheld condition cannot supply by itself. Third, add a random-example condition drawn from the same pool, which varies example relevance while holding example presence fixed and would separate the benefit of a similar example from the benefit of any example. Fourth, repeat runs per condition, so that decoding non-determinism and session-to-session power variation can be separated from genuine differences, and extend the resampling to the curated set and to the Spider and BIRD estimates, neither of which carries intervals in this draft; the withheld-pool-retrieval-against-fixed-examples differences now do carry template-clustered intervals, and what they need instead is more templates, since four of the six remain unresolved on 26 clusters. A BIRD timeout-exclusion sensitivity analysis and a per-condition error taxonomy for the two three-shot conditions belong in the same pass. The control that would have mattered most — retrieval that excludes the target question's own template — is already in the design and is reported in Section 6.2.

# 9. Conclusion

This paper set out a procedure for measuring text-to-SQL correctness and device energy together on an 8 GB edge board, and applied it to six small open-weight models (1B to 4B parameters, Q4_K_M) on a Jetson Orin Nano Super across a local taxi database, a curated question set, and the Spider and BIRD public benchmarks. Its primary products are the procedure, the energy-per-correct-answer figures it produces, and the estimates and intervals attached to each comparison it makes.

Two accuracy findings carry the most weight. First, having examples in the prompt matters more than the choice among these models: three-shot prompting with retrieved similar examples lifted taxi accuracy from {{qc15-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}–{{qc3-q4-ollama_nyc0_ex}}% to {{prem1b-q4-ours_nycsim_ex}}–{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycsim_ex}}% for every model, with paired cluster-bootstrap intervals far from zero. Those pooled lifts are the midpoint of two conditional lifts, fixed at that midpoint by the design's equal split between covered and withheld pools rather than by anything a deployment would experience: for QwenCoder-3B the lift is {{qc3-q4-ollama_lift_covered}} pp covered and {{qc3-q4-ollama_lift_withheld}} pp withheld, pooling to {{qc3-q4-ollama_3shot_lift}} pp. Fixed generic examples are weaker against zero-shot and not uniform: five of the six clustered intervals exclude zero and Qwen3-4B's, at [{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_lo}}, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_fixlift_ci_hi}}] pp, does not. That exception has an identified cause: {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_n}} of Qwen3-4B's {{n_nyc_eval}} fixed-example answers ({{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycfix_top_error_pct}}%) fail on one repeated column-name error that no other model commits once, so that condition's score is a failure mode rather than a capability estimate. Retrieval's advantage over those generic examples, however, is largely contingent on coverage: with the item's own template withheld, the paired, template-clustered advantage separates from zero for two of the six models — {{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{prem1b-q4-ours_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}] for Prem-1B-SQL and {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_pt}} pp [{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_lo}}, {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_retr_adv_unc_ci_hi}}] for Qwen3-4B, the latter attributable in large part to that error — and is unresolved for the other four, whose intervals all include zero. This study therefore does not establish similarity-based example selection as the generally stronger strategy once structural coverage is removed, and it equally does not establish it as the weaker one for any model. Second, a substantial share of the retrieval benefit is associated with the example pool covering the question's structure. Each item was measured both against an example pool containing its own query template and against a pool from which that template was withheld; accuracy falls from {{prem1b-q4-ours_seen_ex}}–{{qc3-q4-ollama_seen_ex}}% to {{prem1b-q4-ours_unseen_ex}}–{{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_unseen_ex}}%, a within-item gap of {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_gen_gap}}–{{llama32-3b-q4-ollama_gen_gap}} pp whose cluster-bootstrap interval excludes zero for every model. Approximately {{template_share_mean}}% of the zero-shot-to-covered-template improvement ({{template_share_min}}–{{template_share_max}}% across models) is associated with the difference between covered and withheld example pools; measured against the pooled S-similar headline, which averages the two exposures equally, the same component is {{template_share_pooled_mean}}% ({{template_share_pooled_min}}–{{template_share_pooled_max}}%). The practical consequence is that retrieval gains measured on a generated question set with a matching example pool will overstate performance on genuinely novel query structures, and that a validated example pool pays off to the extent that incoming questions resemble what is already in it.

Energy per correct answer under retrieval ranges from {{qc15-q4-ollama_nycsim_j_per_correct}} J to {{qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_nycsim_j_per_correct}} J across the six artifacts, so the configuration with the highest observed accuracy is not the cheapest per correct answer. The extremes of that range separate — QwenCoder-1.5B's template-clustered interval lies wholly below Qwen3-4B's — while adjacent configurations such as QwenCoder-3B and XiYanSQL-3B overlap almost entirely, so this study establishes no complete energy ordering, and the intervals cover workload variation only.

The model comparison is a partial order rather than a verdict. Across all {{n_model_pairs}} pairs under retrieval, estimated per item and clustered by template, {{n_model_pairs_separated}} separate after Holm adjustment — Prem-1B-SQL against QwenCoder-3B and against XiYanSQL-3B, both at Holm p < 1e-4 and both with family-simultaneous intervals excluding zero — several more have per-pair intervals excluding zero without surviving adjustment, and the closest pairs are indistinguishable, the top two (QwenCoder-3B against Qwen3-4B) differing by {{contrast_qc3-q4-ollama__qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_diff}} pp with a simultaneous interval of [{{contrast_qc3-q4-ollama__qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_slo}}, {{contrast_qc3-q4-ollama__qwen3-4b-q4-ollama_shi}}] pp. The Spider-to-taxi rank correlation over six models is {{spider_nyc_spearman}} (p = {{spider_nyc_spearman_p}}), which documents a reversal in these artifacts without establishing a general one. Readers choosing a model for a private database should run the procedure on their own schema and questions rather than read an ordering out of this or any benchmark table.

The boundary of this evidence should be stated plainly rather than left to be inferred. Every interval-backed accuracy result above rests on one database, one scored session, and 26 query templates resampled as 26 exchangeable clusters; the Spider, BIRD and curated-set accuracies carry no interval of any kind, and the energy intervals describe variation across the workload rather than across runs. That is enough to establish the two treatment effects reported here, because both are large and measured within items, and it is not enough to settle a ranking among artifacts that sit a few points apart, nor to license extrapolation to other schemas, other question styles, or other hardware. Widening the template inventory, adding databases, and repeating the session are the measurements that would change what can be concluded, and none of them is a matter of further analysis of the data already collected.

Controlled precision comparison and on-device fine-tuning are reserved for a companion study on the same infrastructure; neither is measured here. The protocol, raw outputs, scoring and analysis code, and manuscript source are being prepared for release so that every number above can be regenerated.

# Reproducibility and Data Availability

The project retains the protocol, frozen dataset and model records, raw outputs, scoring code, environment snapshots, and SHA-256 fingerprint tools, with exploratory, pilot, and dry-run material kept separate from the main campaign.
